Research

My work joins statistical theory to biomedical and population health data. The through-line is uncertainty: how to quantify it honestly when predictors outnumber observations, and how to evaluate predictive models so that reported performance survives contact with new data.

01

High-dimensional inference

My dissertation, Inference Using Debiased Fused Lasso, addresses inference in structured high-dimensional regression. The lasso and fused lasso make estimation possible when predictors are numerous and correlated, but the regularization that buys that tractability introduces bias that invalidates standard confidence intervals and hypothesis tests. I develop debiasing procedures for this setting and establish their theoretical and empirical properties. The methods apply directly to correlated biomarkers, ordered molecular features, and other structured measurements where independence assumptions fail. Next: extending these results to survival outcomes, generalized linear models, and longitudinal data.

02

Multi-omics and clinical text

In a study currently under review, I combined BioBERT-derived representations of clinical text with sparse principal components of multi-omics data to predict breast cancer survival, evaluated under a strictly leakage-controlled cross-validation protocol in which every representation-learning step is refit inside each training fold. The finding is that a substantial share of the performance reported in this literature reflects evaluation design rather than biological signal — which matters for any model considered for clinical use.

03

Population health

I am a collaborator in the Global Burden of Disease Study and a named author on GBD 2023 analyses of HIV/AIDS, enteric infectious diseases, heart failure, and tetanus, covering 204 countries and territories across more than three decades. My first-author work applies penalized regression to household-level determinants of child malnutrition in Bangladesh. Earlier, I analyzed national survey data for UNICEF, USAID, and the Bangladesh Bureau of Statistics, including the Multiple Indicator Cluster Survey of roughly 64,000 households.

Teaching

Students should understand why a statistical method is appropriate before they learn how to implement it.

When I introduce linear regression, I begin with scatterplots — trend, slope, intercept, and what a relationship between two variables actually means — before any formula appears. Computation follows understanding rather than substituting for it. I use R and Python throughout, but I insist that software output be interpreted critically: a p-value is the beginning of an argument, not the end of one. A student who can run a regression but cannot say what it assumes has not yet learned regression.

My research supplies the data. Public GBD estimates let an introductory class chart disease mortality since 1990 and compute change across decades, while an upper-division class extends the same data to multiple regression and uncertainty intervals. What I value most is the discussion these force: counts versus rates, why age-standardization matters, and why an observed trend is not a cause.
